## How to read this document

Each hypothesis states: the **claim**, **why it matters**, the **confirm conditions**, the **falsify conditions**, the **evidence that would decide it**, and the **decision it drives**. A hypothesis is only decided by the evidence named — not by opinion, optimism, or a broker's assertion.

**Statuses used honestly:**
- **UNTESTED** — no target-specific evidence exists yet; the hypothesis can only be tested in diligence or after opening.
- **SUPPORTED (by the report's general evidence)** — the master report's market or industry evidence points in the claim's direction, but no target-specific test has been run.
- **CONTRADICTED (by the report's general evidence)** — the master report's evidence points against the claim as stated; the hypothesis must be re-specified or abandoned.
- **PARTIALLY SUPPORTED** — some elements are evidenced, others are not.

**No probabilities are stated anywhere in this document.** The master report gives no probabilities for these outcomes, and none is invented here. Where a threshold is stated (e.g., a volume or margin figure), it is a decision threshold taken from the report's model, not a probability.

**What is carried forward unresolved:** the report's **23 unverified items** (§A.9), **10 attorney questions** (§3.5), and **12 missing owner inputs** (§5.8) — listed in full in SWOT.md §5. Several hypotheses cannot be tested until specific owner inputs (notably available capital, credit band, and whether the owner is a NY-licensed pharmacist) are supplied.

## H1 — Acquisition thesis: an acquired book of business retains volume and PBM contracts

|---|---|

| **Why it matters** | This is the central claim behind "acquire, do not build." ## H2 — Volume ramp: the target's Rx/day sustains or grows after closing

| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| **Claim** | After closing, the acquired store's prescription volume sustains at (or grows from) its diligence baseline, reaching at least the break-even volume for the acquired store's cost base — and, by analogy to the startup model, at least the Standard-scenario reference of 111 Rx/day break-even against a 150 Rx/day steady-state target — within the first year of ownership. |
| **Why it matters** | Volume is the second-ranked sensitivity in the model (§5.5): every month of delay at <100 Rx/day costs ~$12K–$19K of EBITDA in Scenario B, and a 3-month slip extends the cash trough past the working-capital reserve. If volume falls after closing, the owner is paying goodwill plus rent plus a full-time PIC for a shrinking file. |
| **Confirm conditions** | (a) Diligence baseline: 3-year monthly script counts from the dispensing system, stable or growing, with seasonality and the month any decline started identified; (b) post-closing tracking (first 30 days, then weekly): script counts, new-vs.-refill mix, and GP/script tracked against the diligence baseline (§10.7, task 6.3); (c) the retention drivers are in place: incumbent PIC and key staff stay, location/phone/hours/delivery/language unchanged, seller transition period completed, joint patient letter (seller + buyer) sent before closing; (d) no single prescriber/facility concentration that could remove volume in one event. |
| **Falsify conditions** | (a) Diligence shows a declining 3-year script trend; (b) post-closing weekly script counts fall materially below the diligence baseline for a sustained period (the report sets no numeric retention threshold — the CPA must set the trigger from the target's actuals; no threshold is invented here); (c) the incumbent PIC or a top prescriber leaves and volume follows; (d) a material payer's recredentialing fails or a network change forces patients to transfer. |
| **Evidence that would decide it** | Diligence: monthly script counts for 3 years from the dispensing system; top-10 prescribers by volume and % of scripts; top facilities/contracts and renewal dates; transfer history (scripts lost/gained monthly); active patient count and new-vs.-refill mix (§9.4A–B). Post-closing: week-1 and week-4 trading reports vs. the diligence baseline (§10.7, task 6.3); monthly management pack from the CPA (§10.7, task 6.4). Benchmarks for context (not targets for a specific store): national average 67,601 prescriptions in 2024 (~217/day) and 65,795 in 2025 (NCPA Digests); the startup model's steady-state targets are 80 / 150 / 220 Rx/day (A/B/C) with break-evens of 68 / 111 / 171 Rx/day (§1.4, §5.5). **Do not apply the startup projections to a target** — the equivalent projections for an acquisition must be built from the target's actuals (§11.7). |
| **Decision it drives** | If confirmed: the acquisition thesis (H1) holds on volume; hold the working-capital reserve as sized and proceed with the growth plan. If falsified in diligence: walk away or re-price only on a quantified exposure (see H1). If falsified post-closing: the CPA re-forecasts from actuals, the owner and PIC diagnose the cause (prescriber loss, payer change, staffing, communication), and the seller note/earn-out — if negotiated — absorbs part of the shortfall; if volume cannot be restored, the owner must decide whether to hold, restructure, or exit on counsel's and the CPA's advice. |
| **Current status** | **UNTESTED.** No target exists yet. The report's general evidence is mixed: the mechanism (retention driven by pharmacist staying, prescriber engagement, communication quality) is supported by the diligence literature cited (§9.5), but **no pharmacy-specific retention percentage was found** — any precise "% retained after a pharmacy sale" figure should be treated as invented unless sourced. The UK clinic migration example (well-handled transitions lost under 5%, badly handled 20%+ — Pexpo) illustrates a principle about communication quality; it is **not** a U.S. pharmacy-sale retention rate. |

## H3 — Margin: front-end + clinical services carry the profit dispensing cannot

| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| **Claim** | The acquired store can earn enough profit outside PBM-priced dispensing — front-end/OTC sales, immunisations, medication synchronisation, adherence packaging, and medication therapy management (MTM) — to make the business viable despite dispensing losing money at the national average. |
| **Why it matters** | This is the economic core of the whole enterprise. At the national average, dispensing loses $0.43 per prescription ($14.57 gross profit vs. $15.00 cost to dispense, 2024; NCPA 2025 Digest). If no non-dispensing profit exists, the store cannot be viable at any volume — more volume on below-cost contracts grows losses (§8.4 Risk 1). |
| **Confirm conditions** | (a) The target's diligence shows a revenue and gross-profit split — dispensing vs. front-end vs. clinical services (vaccines, MTM, compounding, LTC, specialty) — with front-end and clinical gross profit separately positive and material (§9.4A); (b) the immunisation programme is operable: at least one SED-certified immuniser on duty for every session, a standing (non-patient-specific) order signed by a NY-licensed physician or nurse practitioner, a private area for immunisation, CPR/BLS current, approved immunisation course within the past 3 years, NYSIIS/CIR reporting in place, and the correct billing rail identified per vaccine (Part B roster billing vs. Part D vs. commercial/Medicaid); (c) med-sync/adherence packaging operates with a posted service fee disclosed before the service starts (fee set with the CPA); (d) any MTM fee is obtained in writing from the payer/platform before it is forecast. |
| **Falsify conditions** | (a) The target's GP split shows front-end and clinical services are negligible or loss-making and the store's profit depends entirely on dispensing spread; (b) no certified immuniser is available, or the standing order / private area / registry reporting cannot be put in place; (c) the immunisation billing rail cannot be established (e.g., the pharmacy is not enrolled as a mass immuniser for Part B roster billing); (d) the CPA's forecast from the target's actual contract rates shows the store is not viable without assuming a PBM rate increase, PBM reform, or a cost-plus offer not yet made in writing. |
| **Evidence that would decide it** | Diligence: revenue/GP split by stream (§9.4A); the target's actual PBM contract rates for its top 50 drugs, modelled by the PIC/CPA (§8.2); wholesaler acquisition costs vs. PBM MACs for the top generics; immunisation SOP, standing order, certification records, and registry reporting; MTM platform contract and fee (if any). Model anchors for context: front-end sales 2% (A) / 3% (B) / 4% (C) of Rx revenue at 45% front-end gross margin — both ASSUMPTIONS (§5.1; basis: NCPA Digest states >96% of sales are prescription department). **No MTM fee, packaging fee, or front-store margin figure is stated in the report** — obtain contract rates and wholesaler data before forecasting any of them. The 2026 Part B immunisation administration fee of $34.62 in one secondary source is **not verified from a CMS fee schedule** — obtain the current CMS fee before forecasting immunisation revenue. |
| **Decision it drives** | If confirmed: the store's profit engine is the services, and the operating plan prioritises them from month 1 (immunisation clinics, med sync, adherence packaging, MTM) with separate tracking of front-store gross profit vs. prescription gross profit (§8.2). If falsified: the target is priced as a dispensing-only business — which at the national average loses money — so either the price must reflect that (a quantified discount, in writing from the accountant) or the owner walks away. |
| **Current status** | **PARTIALLY SUPPORTED (by general evidence) / UNTESTED (for any target).** Supported: the *authority* for pharmacist immunisation in NY is verified (Educ. Law §§6527, 6801, 6909; 8 NYCRR §63.9), and the *mechanism* (administration fee can exceed the cost of the pharmacist's time if billed correctly, enrolled as a mass immuniser, and run at volume) is stated in the report (§8.2). Untested: whether any specific target actually earns material non-dispensing profit — the report states no verified Brooklyn front-store margin and no MTM or packaging fee. The claim as a *forecast* for a specific store is therefore untested. |

## H4 — PBM economics: reimbursement pressure stays within the modeled band

| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| **Claim** | For the acquired store, PBM reimbursement pressure stays within the band the model assumes: gross margin between 18.2% (conservative, VERIFIED 2024 figure, 10-year low) and 20.9% (base, VERIFIED 2025 figure), with revenue per Rx between $75.00 and $79.20 — so that the store's break-even volume stays at or below the level its volume can sustain. (For reference, the startup model's base break-evens are 68 / 111 / 171 Rx/day at $79.20 and 20.9%; at 18.2% margin they rise to 78 / 127 / 194 Rx/day.) |
| **Why it matters** | Margin is the #1 sensitivity in the model (§5.5): at base revenue ($79.20), dropping margin from 20.9% to 18.2% lifts break-even by 10 (A), 16 (B), and 23 (C) Rx/day. Raising revenue/Rx from $79.20 to $85.00 at base margin only cuts break-even by 4–11 Rx/day. **PBM reimbursement / generic-vs-brand mix is the #1 sensitivity — not footfall.** If margin falls below 18.2%, the Lean build never breaks even and the Larger build is cumulatively EBITDA-negative after three years. |
| **Confirm conditions** | (a) The PIC/CPA model the network's actual paid amounts for the store's expected volume **before** enrolling in any Part D network, drug by drug, and confirm no material drug is stocked at a reimbursement below acquisition cost + the cost to dispense; (b) the target's GP/script by payer is verified positive on the material contracts (§9.4A); (c) the PBM/PSAO contracts are read before signing (attorney review): reimbursement methodology, MAC update/appeal terms, GER/BER target/reconciliation period/cap, spread pricing, audit/recoupment rights, termination and rate-change rights; (d) DIR-fee amounts, timing, and accruals are quantified in dollars, and EBITDA is stated **after** proper DIR accrual (§9.4A); (e) remittances are reconciled claim-by-claim (835 matched to the dispensing system) weekly from day one. |
| **Falsify conditions** | (a) A material PBM contract reimburses below acquisition cost on a material share of scripts; (b) margin on the target's actual payer mix falls below 18.2% on a sustained basis; (c) a GER/BER reconciliation claws back a material amount from a future remittance; (d) a cost-plus offer, if made, requires the wholesaler's confidential invoices to verify "dead-net" acquisition cost with off-invoice discounts stripped out, and the attorney/CPA model shows it is worse than the MAC-based rate; (e) a PBM changes a rate unilaterally without notice and without a termination right (a contract term the report says never to sign — §8.2). |
| **Evidence that would decide it** | Diligence: GP/script by payer; DIR-fee amounts/timing/accruals; every PBM/PSAO contract; the target's actual remittance data (835s) matched to adjudicated claims; wholesaler invoices for MAC-appeal evidence. Post-closing: weekly PBM reconciliation; monthly GP/script-by-payer report; batch MAC appeals with invoices. Context figures: Part D dispensing fees $0.00–$7.15 (mean ~$0.65) vs. ~$15.00 cost to dispense (Pharmacy Times, June 2026 — direction established, precise mean to be confirmed against the plan's own remittance data); 96.5% of independents said Part D reimbursement threatens viability (NCPA Jan 2025 survey). **No rate is stated in the report for any commercial PBM** — never forecast a per-prescription profit; obtain the actual PBM contract rates for the store's top 50 drugs and model those. |
| **Decision it drives** | If confirmed: the store trades inside its modeled band; the operating disciplines (know the rate before filling, batch MAC appeals, PSAO membership, do-not-stock-below-cost rule) hold. If falsified: the CPA re-forecasts from the actual contract rates; the owner decides, drug by drug and network by network, whether to fill, appeal, or exit a network — and whether the acquisition price still holds. A store whose profit depends on a single PBM/payer is priced lower for a reason (§9.3). |
| **Current status** | **CONTRADICTED (as a claim about the national average) / UNTESTED (for any target).** Contradicted as a general claim: at the national average in 2024, gross profit was $14.57/Rx vs. a $15.00 cost to dispense — the dispensing act itself lost money, and margin was 18.2%, the 10-year low (§8.4 Risk 1). The 2025 figure (20.9%) is a recovery, not a floor: the 10-year series is volatile (19.7% in 2023, 23.3% in 2021). **Do not model 20.9% as a floor** (§5.7). For any specific target, the hypothesis is untested until its GP/script-by-payer is verified. |
