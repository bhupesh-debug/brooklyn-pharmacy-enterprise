# Viewpoint — Brooklyn Pharmacy Enterprise

*The investor's view: what this is, why it might work, what would kill it, and what would change the answer. Written for the green card holder who would own and operate the pharmacy.*

## The opportunity in one page

Brooklyn has 2.74 million people, one-third of New York City's Medicaid enrollees, and — since Rite Aid's second bankruptcy in May 2025 — zero Rite Aid stores. Independent pharmacies survive in the borough where chains have retreated, and the best-scoring neighbourhoods (East New York–Starrett City 78.5, East Flatbush 75.0, Brownsville–Ocean Hill 74.5) are the ones with older, sicker, more Medicaid-dependent populations and thin pharmacy coverage. A green card holder can own 100% of a New York retail pharmacy without a pharmacist licence, provided a full-time licensed pharmacist-in-charge runs the dispensary. The business to buy is not a start-up: it is an existing book of prescriptions, existing PBM contracts, and existing patients, bought at 2.5–3.5× adjusted EBITDA plus inventory at cost.

The catch is that the core act — dispensing a prescription — loses money at the national average: $14.57 of gross profit against a $15.00 cost to dispense. A pharmacy that only dispenses is a business that slowly bleeds. The money has to come from what surrounds dispensing: front-end sales, delivery, medication synchronisation, adherence services, immunisations, and long-term-care work — and from buying the volume instead of building it, so the fixed costs are covered from day one rather than month ten.

Two regulatory facts dominate everything else. First, SBA financing is closed to this owner: since 1 March 2026 a green card holder may not own any share of an SBA 7(a), 504, or Microloan borrower, even 1%. The cheapest, longest-term financing a pharmacy normally relies on is gone, and a conventional bank will want 20–30% down where an SBA loan would have wanted 10%. Second, New York has frozen new pharmacy Medicaid enrollments — including changes of ownership — until at least 27 January 2027, and the freeze can be extended in six-month blocks. Medicaid is roughly a third of the revenue in the target neighbourhoods. Neither fact kills the deal. Both change its price, its timing, and its financing — which is why the verdict is conditional, not clean.

## The thesis

1. **Buy volume, don't build it.** A start-up ramps from zero prescriptions while paying full rent, payroll, and inventory carrying costs; the model shows monthly EBITDA turning positive only in months 8–10 of Year 1, and Lean never breaks even in the conservative variant. An acquired store starts at its steady-state volume on day one — the simulation puts its median time to break-even at one month.
2. **Pay for earnings, not for hope.** The discipline is the multiple: 2.5–3.5× adjusted EBITDA plus inventory at cost, validated against the BizBuySell record (average 2.79×, 2025 median sale $600,000). Every dollar above the band is a dollar the dispensing economics cannot repay — the what-to-avoid list prices it: paying 5× instead of 3× on $200,000 of normalised EBITDA overpays by $400,000.
3. **The pharmacist-in-charge is the business.** The owner cannot dispense, verify, or counsel. The single most valuable asset after the script file is a named, licensed, full-time pharmacist-in-charge under contract before any money moves. Without that person, there is no pharmacy — only a lease and a shelf of stock.
4. **Financing is the constraint, not the idea.** With SBA closed, the deal has to work on personal capital, conventional bank debt, non-SBA CDFI loans, or seller financing. That raises the cash required and narrows the buyer pool — which is also why a disciplined buyer can find a motivated seller.
5. **Medicaid is a timing question, not a thesis question.** If the moratorium lifts on schedule, the acquired store's Medicaid enrollment transfers and the revenue follows. If it extends, the buyer waits — the roadmap hard-pauses at Day 60 and no deposit is at risk. The thesis survives a delay; it does not survive paying for Medicaid revenue that never arrives.

## The numbers that carry the thesis

| Test | Result |
|---|---|
| Break-even volume (deterministic) | 68 / 111 / 171 Rx/day (Lean / Standard / Larger) |
| Acquisition median time to break-even (Monte Carlo, base) | 1 month (P5–P95: 1–13) |
| Acquisition median 5-year ROI (Monte Carlo, base) | 129.6% (P5–P95: 24.3%–240.9%) |
| Acquisition P(payback beyond 5 years) | 33.2% |
| Standard startup median 5-year ROI (Monte Carlo, base) | 149.7% — higher than acquisition, but from zero volume |
| Lean startup P(payback beyond 5 years) | 61.6% — the weakest route |
| Bear variant, acquisition P(never break even) | 45.6% — a bear-case acquisition is a diligence test, not a safe harbour |
| Overlays that kill every route | −20% volume drop; −2pp PBM rate cut; 12-month moratorium extension pushes Lean past the 5-year payback horizon |

The simulation's honest message: the acquisition is the best risk-adjusted route, not a safe one. One in three base-case acquisitions still takes more than five years to pay back, and the bear case punishes every route. Diligence is the product — the purchase price, the script-file retention, and the PBM contract terms decide the outcome more than the neighbourhood does.

## What would change the verdict

**To a clean GO:** the moratorium lifts on schedule with written confirmation that a change of ownership will be processed; a target is found inside the valuation band with verified script volume at or above its break-even; a pharmacist-in-charge signs before the LOI; and non-SBA financing closes at workable terms. Any one of those four, confirmed in writing, moves a gate from conditional to cleared.

**To a NO-GO:** the moratorium is extended twice (past mid-2027) with no path for ownership changes; no pharmacist-in-charge can be recruited at a wage the model supports; the only available targets price above 3.5× without verifiable earnings to justify it; or the owner's available capital cannot cover the down payment plus the working-capital reserve without SBA. A NO-GO on the acquisition route is also a NO-GO on the start-up route — the start-up has the same financing and moratorium problems with worse volume risk.

**To a different deal:** naturalisation would restore SBA eligibility and change the financing entirely — but it is a personal immigration decision, not a financing plan, and no deal should be priced around a future naturalisation date. A citizen co-owner added nominally to restore SBA eligibility is not a different deal; it is a compliance problem.

## What to watch first

The five gates, in order: the moratorium's written position; the target at a disciplined price; the pharmacist-in-charge in writing; financing without SBA; attorney and CPA engaged before the LOI. The first five actions are in `RECOMMENDATION.md`. The first thing the owner must supply is the twelve missing inputs in `JOB_MASTERY.md` §8 — above all, available capital and credit profile, because those two numbers decide whether any of this is affordable before a single pharmacy is toured.
